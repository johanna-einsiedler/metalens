# Everything a reader needs to judge each task

You are given one paper's text in numbered chunks (P1, P2 …, each a run of consecutive
sentences on one page) and the list of its tasks, with ids. For every task, pick EVERY chunk
a reader needs in order to judge the task on the four complexity dimensions of the rubric
below, and to know what was done: what participants had to produce or decide, the materials
and instructions, the steps, the time allowed, the knowledge it took, who else was involved,
how the work was judged, the examples the authors give. Chunks may come from the methods,
the results, the appendix or the discussion. Be exhaustive rather than selective: a reader
will judge the task from the chunks you pick and nothing else, so a chunk that adds any
detail about the task belongs in; leave out chunks that only report numbers, the literature
or the authors' interpretation. For each chunk say which dimensions it informs
(`what_was_done` for the plain description).

Each task is scored on four binary (Yes/No) dimensions from the four descriptive fields of its extraction (`knowledge_education`, `goal_path_specification`, `interdependence`, `resources_context`), never from the paper's own framing of the task and never from its grading structure. A task is **Complex** when it scores Yes on at least three of the four dimensions; otherwise it is **Simple**. The model's scorer sees the four descriptions and nothing else of the paper; a coder scores from the paper itself and marks the sentences each score rests on.

### Dimension 1: Knowledge and Education

Yes — the task requires second-year undergraduate knowledge or above. That is at least some degree of specialization within knowledge domain.

No — high-school or first-year undergraduate knowledge suffices, including basic undergraduate concepts.

Scoring notes: Score from the Knowledge & education field of the extraction. If scaffolding substantially lowered the bar (worked examples, cheat-sheets, procedure-recall tools), score based on what participants actually had to bring after the scaffolding.
Programming note: it is the depth of programming knowledge that matters, not the language name. Intro courses teach Python, Java, or C — but professional uses often require more than basics to use these languages effectively. Do not score a language as first-year simply because it appears in introductory curricula.

Scored from the `knowledge_education` field.

### Dimension 2: Goal Definition and Path Specification

Yes — the path to completion is hard to infer: it is not obvious how to proceed from the stated objective, OR there are many valid approaches / no single right answer, so it is easy to get lost or commit to a wrong route. Importantly, in getting lost there should be a mistake that makes the task fail. This includes tasks whose end-state and metric are perfectly clear but whose route is undiscovered and tasks where it is hard to identify what the objective even is. For example "improve firm profit," "get rich," "win the game", are all tasks whose route is hard to discover and the wrong path would cause a failure (e.g. company default or losing the game).

No — how to proceed is easy to infer, even if the instruction does not spell it out: a determinate done-state with an obvious or readily-found route and little risk of getting lost (a defined deliverable against a key or rubric, a specific problem with a specified target).

Scoring notes: Score from the Goal & path specification field. Subjective or holistic grading is evidence of openness but not sufficient on its own — a concrete problem with an easy-to-infer route, rated holistically (e.g. "resolve this customer's issue," scored by CSAT), is still No. A clean outcome metric does not make a task closed if the route to it is hard to infer.

Scored from the `goal_path_specification` field.

### Dimension 3: Task Interdependence

Yes — the task combines genuinely *different* kinds of subtask, AND an error in an early or upstream component is *hard to catch as the work proceeds* and *likely to propagate or be costly to fix* — often forcing a partial restart rather than a local patch. The heart of the dimension is that mistakes are hard to detect mid-task and expensive to unwind, not that recovery is strictly impossible.

No — the work is either (a) a stream of independent or repeated homogeneous units (one chat after another, paragraph after paragraph), where an error stays local; or (b) coupled but where errors are *easy to catch and cheap to fix as you go* — a counterpart redirects you, you rerun a test, you see intermediate output and revise before completion.

Scoring notes: Score from the Interdependence field. Three rules:
- Trivial decomposition is not interdependence. "You must do A before B" holds almost everywhere and is not the target. Score Yes only when the parts are of different nature and an early error is genuinely hard to spot and costly to undo.
- Easy, timely correction defeats coupling. Fast, reliable feedback (a counterpart who corrects you, a rerunnable test, visible intermediate results) → No. Slow, noisy, or confounded feedback (a market signal weeks later) does not count.
- Programming tasks: distinguish compile-time from deployment feedback. Compilers/tests catch syntax and unit-level errors quickly, but multi-day/multi-week projects surface the relevant feedback at integration or deployment — slow and noisy. A 1-hour task with a rerunnable test suite → No; an ongoing project where errors surface at launch or in downstream systems → Yes.
- Do not score from the grading structure. A conjunctive grade ("pass all 12") or a single holistic score is a measurement property, not task structure; ignore it.

Scored from the `interdependence` field.

### Dimension 4: Resources and Context

Yes — the task requires substantial resources beyond what is handed to the participant: extensive materials to search or synthesise across a large context (e.g. a corpus of documents, multiple studies to browse, a large codebase, browsing the internet for at least an hour), specialist data sources or tools, or substantial context-specific knowledge — whether firm-specific (e.g. tacit knowledge), study-specific, procedural, or otherwise — that the participant must bring or that would need to be supplied to a model.

No — the task is self-contained: a few materials sitting directly in front of the participant suffice, and it draws only on general knowledge or basic everyday tacit knowledge.

Scoring notes: Score from the Resources & context field. Do not count the AI tool being studied as a resource — it is the treatment.

Scored from the `resources_context` field.
